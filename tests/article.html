A memoization layer for the Saad–Lee sampler that runs up to 59× faster, produces byte-identical output, and spends exactly the same random bits.

Code: github.com/tshiven/Caching-Random-Variate
Built on librvg by Feras A. Saad and Wonyeol Lee

01

The randomness that isn't truly random

Most programming languages have a function that hands you a random number from a given distribution. Nevertheless, almost none of them give you a truly random number.

Instead you get an approximation: a curve with gaps where certain values can never appear, and tails that thin out at a different rate than the math prescribes. It comes from pushing a uniform number through arithmetic that inherits rounding errors at every step.

In most software the difference is unmeasurable, but there are applications where that difference is the entire problem.

Machine learning and simulations are the everyday version. Training runs and test simulations often can't be reproduced because the random seed behaviour depends on the machine.

Cryptography raises the stakes further: a key generator with even a faint bias makes it more vulnerable, and floating-point rounding often introduces such biases.

Underneath all of these bugs sits one structural problem. Classical sampling algorithms are designed in the Real-RAM model where you can draw a perfectly uniform real number from the unit interval and compute with infinite precision. A physical computer can't do either. It gets a stream of random bits and executes finite-precision arithmetic. The distribution your library actually produces is therefore usually unknown and much different from the distribution expected from the machine.

02

Sampling straight from the CDF

Feras Saad and Wonyeol Lee took a different route to this problem. Rather than repairing samplers one distribution at a time, they changed what the user gives the library in the first place. They published both the method and a C implementation of it, librvg. Everything in this article is a layer sitting on top of what they built.

In their approach you supply one thing: a finite-precision program that computes the distribution's CDF, a function returning the fraction of samples landing at or below a given point. From that alone, a generator is synthesized for you.

Here are the guarantees provided:

  • It is exact. The generator's distribution provably matches the CDF you wrote, at the precision you wrote it in.
  • It is entropy-optimal. It consumes the smallest possible average number of random bits per sample. This is a hard floor set by information theory, so no algorithm can do better.
  • It needs no arbitrary-precision arithmetic and cannot overflow. It works in whatever binary format the CDF uses.
  • Its API is self-consistent. Generator, survival function and quantile function all derive from the same CDF, so they agree with each other by construction rather than by careful maintenance.

Describe the distribution once, as a CDF. The exact sampler is generated for you.

How the sampler walks

The algorithm narrows the answer down one bit at a time. It asks, repeatedly, "is the answer in the lower or upper half?", and each answer fixes one more bit of the result. A double carries 64 bits, so a sample takes up to 64 rounds.

You might expect every round also costs a coin flip, since each one is deciding left or right. Surprisingly, it doesn't. Some rounds get decided by the CDF value alone, no randomness needed, and that gap is what the rest of this article is built around.

Why would a round come out free? Sometimes all the remaining probability sits on one side of the midpoint. Other times the two sides are distinguishable from bits of earlier rounds. In both cases, the generator moves on without consuming randomness, which is why the method is entropy-optimal.

Under the hood

The method's ancestor is Knuth–Yao (1976) which builds a discrete distribution generating tree and is entropy-optimal, but requires creating a tree of exponential size. Saad and Lee's contribution is proving the tree never has to exist. A single sample only needs one root-to-leaf path, and every node on that path can be recomputed from the CDF the moment it's needed. Hence, nothing needs to be stored in advance. Their results go further and remove Knuth–Yao's preprocessing pass too. A depth-k walk therefore costs O(k) work and O(1) memory per level, over a distribution that may have 264 possible outcomes.

So the coin flips are already optimal and cannot be improved on. There is, however, room for improvement elsewhere.

03

The other cost: all those CDF calls

Entropy-optimal does not mean fast. It means frugal with randomness. The runtime is dominated by something else: up to 64 CDF evaluations for every single sample.

A Gaussian CDF is cheap, a single call to a well-behaved function. A Beta CDF is not: it has no closed form, so evaluating it means running an internal iterative computation until it converges. A Poisson CDF is a running sum, with more terms as the parameter grows. A hypergeometric CDF drags in factorials. These aren't one arithmetic operation, they are small numerical algorithms, and the sampler calls one of them at every single round, dozens of times per sample.

That cost adds up fast, and is often neglected. A Bayesian neural network does not learn one fixed value for each weight. Instead it learns a distribution of possible values, and each time the network runs it samples a new weight from that distribution. During every training run, the network repeatedly samples from the same set of learned distributions. A reinforcement learning agent explores its environment by adding random noise to the action it would otherwise take, and that noise comes from a fixed distribution, sampled again at every step.

A diffusion model, like the ones behind image generators, works in a similar repetitive way. It starts with pure noise and gradually turns that noise into an image over dozens or even hundreds of steps. At each step, it samples according to a distribution determined by the same fixed noise schedule.

Across all those repeated calls to one fixed distribution, how much of that work is repetitive?

Most of it, and it repeats in two structurally different ways. This is why the method uses two caches rather than one. The first saves individual CDF calls. The second skips whole samples. Both live in an add-on library, rvg_cache, that leaves librvg untouched, reached through a single function dropped in where the original call used to be.

When it pays off

Caching earns its keep when a workload has three properties.

  • Many samples from one fixed distribution. Building a cache costs a small fixed amount of time on the very first call, and that cost has to be earned back by saving on later calls. For most distributions it's earned back almost immediately, somewhere between 10 and 25 samples. Geometric is the outlier at roughly 200, since its CDF is cheap enough that there is little per-sample cost to recover.
  • Parameters that hold still. Change a shape parameter and you have a different CDF, which means a different cache starting from empty.
  • An expensive CDF. The whole trade is memory for CDF calls, so a CDF that costs three arithmetic operations has nothing worth saving.

In practice this means caching almost never costs you anything. Even a modest workload of a few dozen samples is already on the profitable side.

04

Which distributions benefit, and why

Discrete distributions win enormously. At 100,000 samples, Poisson, Binomial, NegBinomial, Hypergeometric and Pascal land between 33× and 59×. Continuous distributions win modestly, with Beta, Gamma, Chi-square, F and Student's t settling between 1.34× and 1.47×.

The reason is structural. A discrete distribution has a limited set of possible answers; a Poisson(5) mostly returns small integers. Thousands of different random routes result into the same handful of endings, hence a stored ending gets reused constantly. A continuous distribution produces a unique value on every draw, so its endings are visited once and never again. Continuous distributions consequently get only the weaker of the two caches, described below.

Ten distributions are deliberately absent. Gaussian, exponential, lognormal, Cauchy, Laplace and logistic were measured and caching didn't reliably help, so the library skips it unless you override. Rayleigh, Pareto, Weibull and Gumbel are correctness-tested but never speed-tested.

The gains do not grow forever either. Pushing the sweep past 100,000 samples, the tail-cached distributions level off rather than continuing to climb, since both tables eventually fill and there is no room left to store anything new. Poisson holds near 59× through a million samples.

Every sample, regardless of distribution, starts from the same place: the CDF gets evaluated at a midpoint computed only from the bit path b and the depth l. Same path, same depth, hence same answer. And paths overlap heavily near the root. Every sample tests the same first midpoint, half share the second, a quarter share the third. Only as depth grows does the tree fan out into genuinely unique territory.

That overlap at the top of the tree is the head cache: a hash table keyed on (b, l), holding the CDF value. It has 65,536 slots and never resizes or evicts. A key hashes to a slot; if occupied, the lookup checks up to five more; if all six are taken by other keys, it gives up and calls the CDF directly.

Under the hood

Stored values are raw 32-bit patterns rather than floats. A CDF can legitimately return -0.0 or a NaN, and those must key and compare as distinct entries, so nothing in the cache layer ever compares floats with ==. Everything goes through memcpy-based bit extraction instead.

05

The moment randomness stops mattering

The head cache saves one CDF call at a time. Useful, but bounded, since you still walk all 64 rounds.

The second idea skips a part of the sample entirely, by exploiting the rounds that don't need a coin flip.

Once a walk passes its final coin flip, every round after that is deterministic. The generator keeps computing, but there is nothing left to decide, since the answer is already determined.

After the last coin flip, the rest of the walk is a function. Feed it the same state and it returns the same number every time, because the coin is never consulted again.

So the cache records that state and pairs it with the finished result. Any future sample landing on the same state can jump straight to the answer.

Why this doesn't quietly break anything

A cache hit only skips rounds that use zero coin flips, and without the cache those same rounds would have used zero as well. So the random stream ends up in exactly the state it would have anyway. That's also why the algorithm only stores states after the last flip, and only once the full sample is known: anything earlier still depends on randomness, and the answer doesn't exist until the walk is done.

Under the hood

Every comparison against unmodified librvg checks two things: the raw bit pattern of the result, and the exact coin-flip count. 33 parameterizations, 20,000 seeds each, zero mismatches on both. The flip count is what makes this convincing, since a broken cache would desynchronize the random stream immediately, long before a distributional test would ever catch it.

06

Two engineering decisions worth defending

The loop had to be copied

Both caches need to hook things that happen inside librvg's walk: the CDF call, and the state at each round. C offers no way to intercept a call made inside somebody else's function without editing that function. Since the requirement was that librvg stay untouched, the loop was duplicated instead with the cache calls spliced into the copy.

Only the control flow is duplicated. The arithmetic that determines the sampled value, the exact subtraction, the bit extraction, the float conversions, is not reimplemented. The cache links against the same compiled functions librvg calls.

When caching isn't available, whether the distribution wasn't measured to benefit, the allocation failed, or the registry is full, the code falls back to the exact same uncached path automatically.

The cache made itself invisible

An earlier version handed callers a cache object to create and manage by hand, and mismatching it with the wrong CDF was a real risk, caught only by a runtime check that killed the process. The current version removes that risk instead of just detecting it: caching state is located automatically, so there's no handle left to mismatch, and cleanup happens on its own.

What happens when the table fills

The head cache is fixed at 65,536 slots and never grows. Chi-square and Gamma fill theirs entirely within the tested range, Chi-square by 25,000 samples and Gamma by 50,000. After that, many new lookups find no room at all.

The speed keeps increasing anyway. The reason is arrival order: the slots that fill first hold the shallow, high-traffic states that nearly every sample passes through, and those keep paying off indefinitely. What arrives later, once the table is full, tends to be rare deep states a typical sample would hit once or twice at most, so losing them costs little. A failed lookup is cheap too, at most six slot checks before falling back, a fixed worst-case cost rather than one that grows as the table fills.

Under the hood

The occupancy and hit-rate figures come from a separate instrumented run, since counting entries and hits changes the timing slightly. Within that run, speedup continued to climb past saturation: Chi-square from 1.244× to 1.270×, Gamma from 1.285× to 1.327×.

08

Where this does not work

  • Single-threaded only. All cache state is shared, so calling this from multiple threads at once is not safe.
  • The cache tracks CDFs by memory address, not by what they compute. Two separate functions that calculate the same distribution still get two separate caches, since the system can't tell they are mathematically identical.
  • 64 distinct CDFs maximum, located by a linear scan. Past the cap, new CDFs simply run uncached with no error raised, and that scan itself adds a small amount of per-call overhead as the number of distributions grows.
  • One machine, one compiler. Every number here comes from a single Apple Silicon machine under Apple Clang. Speedups may differ on other hardware or with a different compiler.
09

Where this could go next

  • Parameter-aware caching. Today a Gamma(2,3) and a Gamma(2.01,3) share nothing at all.
  • The middle of the walk is still uncovered. The head cache handles the start and the tail cache handles the end. Everything in between stays uncached: those rounds are too specific to be worth storing individually, and still consume randomness, so neither trick applies.
  • The tuning is still manual. Which distributions get a tail cache, and how deep the head cache reaches, were both set by hand from measurements. A short profiling pass at startup could pick those settings per workload instead.
